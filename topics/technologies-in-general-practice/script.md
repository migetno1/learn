# Technologies in General Practice — presentation script

> **Presentation version:** prepared for a 30–40 minute practice presentation on 23 July 2026. Evidence review current as at 12 July 2026 (Australia).
>
> **Important boundary:** this is education for clinicians, not individual clinical, legal, privacy, procurement or cyber-security advice. Named products illustrate categories; they are not endorsements or comparative recommendations. Clinical, legal/privacy, consumer and Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander review remain outstanding.

## Timing and delivery

- **Target speaking time:** about 35 minutes.
- **Comfortable range:** 32–39 minutes, depending on pauses and audience discussion.
- **Slides:** 14 scenes, matching the live presentation.
- **Short route:** omit the clinician-learning appendix on slide 13 and shorten the audience pauses; this brings the talk closer to 30–32 minutes.
- **Delivery style:** conversational and colleague-to-colleague. The words below are intended to be spoken naturally, not read mechanically.

## Central question

> When does technology genuinely help the patient—and what must remain true for that help to be safe?

---

## Slide 1 — Technology should help the patient

**Time: 1½–2 minutes**

Good evening. I want to look at technology in general practice from a deliberately practical angle. This is not a catalogue of impressive tools, and it is not a talk about whether AI will replace us. The question I want to keep returning to is simpler: **does this technology make care better for the patient, in the real workflow of a general practice?**

For every technology, I will cover four things. First, what it actually does—because “AI” is too broad to be a useful description. Second, what the evidence does and does not show. Third, how it might fit into our practice. And fourth, what could make it fail: poor data, extra work, privacy risk, alert fatigue, inequity, or simply no one being responsible for what happens next.

[Pause; optional show of hands.]

Before we start, who here is already using an AI scribe, an evidence assistant, or another AI-enabled clinical tool?

The key distinction tonight is between **current**, **emerging**, and **research-horizon** technologies. Availability is not the same as evidence of benefit. A polished output is not a patient outcome. And whether software is regulated depends on its intended purpose—not merely on whether the vendor calls it AI.

I am going to follow one fictional patient through the care journey. That gives us a way to ask not only what each tool can produce, but what changes for the patient, the GP and the practice team.

**Transition:** Let us meet Michael before we meet the technology.

---

## Slide 2 — Meet Michael

**Time: 2 minutes**

Michael is fictional. He is 64, self-employed as a school-bus driver and lives outside town. His internet connection is patchy, and leaving his route for repeated appointments is difficult.

He books because his urinary frequency and nocturia are getting worse. He already has BPH, so there is an obvious anchoring risk. The broader story is thirst, fatigue, disrupted sleep and concern about remaining alert on the morning route.

Michael wants a concise written plan, followed by teach-back. He wants fewer unnecessary trips into town. He can also decline any technology, correct his information and choose a phone, paper or ordinary consultation pathway.

Ordinary clinical assessment and investigation later identify type 2 diabetes. That sentence matters: **no AI makes Michael’s diagnosis**. The technology may help him tell the story, preserve the record, surface a care gap or support follow-up, but the history, examination, investigation, interpretation and clinical responsibility remain ours.

As we go, I want us to ask two questions. What problem are we genuinely solving for Michael? And are we solving it—or quietly shifting more work and risk onto him?

**Transition:** The first opportunity occurs before Michael enters the room.

## Slide 3 — Help Michael tell the whole story

**Technology:** adaptive digital pre-consultation history; BetterConsult is the example

**Maturity:** current
**Time: 2½–3 minutes**

### What it is and how it works

From Michael’s side, he receives a link before the appointment and completes a questionnaire on a phone, tablet or computer. The questions adapt to his earlier answers. He can enter the presenting concern, symptoms, medicines and other relevant history in his own time.

From the GP’s side, the responses become a structured patient-entered summary available before or during the consultation. The summary is a starting point. It is not a diagnosis, a complete history or an urgency assessment.

For Michael, “urinary symptoms” can expand into nocturia, thirst, fatigue, sleep disruption and the effect on his work. For the GP, the potential gain is seeing the breadth of the agenda early enough to clarify it rather than discovering the fourth concern as the consultation ends.

### What the evidence says

This is current digital health rather than necessarily generative AI. BetterConsult is in use and has been used as infrastructure in Australian research, including a digital self-screening trial protocol. However, this review did not identify independent evidence that BetterConsult itself improves diagnosis or patient outcomes. A research protocol establishes that a platform can be used in a study; it is not an effectiveness result.

So the defensible claim is modest: structured preparation **may** improve agenda discovery and help some patients remember or disclose concerns. We should not claim that it reliably saves time or improves outcomes without local or independent evidence.

### How this could apply in our practice

The practical use would be targeted rather than universal: longer or complex appointments, new patients, chronic-disease reviews, or presentations where a structured history is helpful. We could start by asking whether the summary changes agenda recognition, duplication, consultation flow or patient experience.

The implementation question is not “Can we send a form?” It is: who reviews it, when is it reviewed, what happens if it contains an urgent answer, and does the information enter the clinical record in a usable way?

### What could stop it working

The patient is doing extra work before the appointment. Non-completion, disability, limited English, low literacy, distress, cost of data and patchy connectivity can all make the tool least accessible to the people who may already face barriers.

A neat summary can also suppress nuance or create false reassurance that the history has already been taken. Michael must still be invited to tell the story in his own words, and we must verify material details and assess urgency independently. Phone, paper and ordinary consultation alternatives need to remain normal—not second-rate exceptions.

**Take-home line:** Pre-consultation technology can widen the doorway into the consultation, but it must never become the price of entry.

## Slide 4 — Preserve attention—and the record

**Technology:** ambient clinical documentation; Lyrebird is a personal example

**Maturity:** current
**Time: 3½–4 minutes**

### What it is and how it works

For the patient, the visible experience may be simple: the GP explains that an ambient scribe will capture the conversation to help draft the note, answers questions, obtains consent and offers ordinary note-taking as an alternative.

Behind that, there are several steps. Audio is captured; speech recognition converts it into text; a language model identifies and summarises clinically relevant content; and the system produces a draft note or related document. Product details vary, including what audio or transcripts are retained, where data are processed, who can access them and whether data can be used for training.

For the doctor, this is not “press record and the note is done”. The workflow is: explain, consent, activate, conduct the consultation, stop, review the whole draft, correct it and only then authorise it into the record. If the content is carried into a referral, care plan or certificate, those outputs also need review.

### What the evidence says

The evidence is promising but not definitive. Systematic reviews of outpatient implementation generally report favourable signals for documentation time, task load or clinician experience in some settings. Manual editing is common, safety evidence is incomplete, and most studies are from the United States rather than Australian general practice.

A 2025 pragmatic US randomised trial of 238 outpatient physicians found that one of two scribes modestly reduced time in notes, while the other did not show the same primary-outcome effect. Both had favourable secondary wellbeing signals. That variation is important: evidence for one tool, version or workflow cannot be transferred automatically to another.

We should therefore say scribes **may** improve attention, documentation burden or after-hours work for some clinicians. We should not promise that a scribe saves a fixed number of minutes, prevents burnout or improves patient outcomes.

### How this could apply in our practice

This is one of the most plausible technologies for a small, governed practice trial. We could pilot it with willing clinicians and patients, a standard consent explanation, a clear non-scribe alternative and a defined review period.

The measures should go beyond “Do we like it?” We could sample notes for omissions, fabricated content, medicine and allergy errors, examination errors and time spent correcting. We could also ask patients whether the technology changed what they were willing to disclose, and ask clinicians whether total documentation time actually changed.

### What could stop it working

The draft can mishear, omit, fabricate, reverse chronology, misattribute a statement or invent an examination finding. It cannot see non-verbal information unless we explicitly document it. A long, fluent note may be harder to check than a short note we wrote ourselves.

Consent and recording requirements depend on the purpose, workflow, practice policy and applicable state or territory law. We should follow current professional guidance and obtain MDO or legal advice for our local setup rather than assuming one consent process applies everywhere.

The other risk is automation bias: clicking approve because the note looks polished. For Michael, I would specifically check that the note preserves thirst, fatigue, sleep disruption and route-safety concerns, and that it does not invent a normal examination.

**Take-home line:** An ambient scribe creates a draft, not a record. The clinician remains the author.

## Slide 5 — Surface a defined care gap

**Technology:** evidence-linked prompts; Primary Sense and Future Health Today are examples

**Maturity:** current
**Time: 2½–3 minutes**

### What it is and how it works

This category is less glamorous than a chatbot, but often more clinically mature. The software extracts structured data from the clinical record—diagnostic codes, medicines, pathology, observations or recorded preventive care—and checks those data against rules, risk logic or guideline-linked algorithms.

For the patient, this may appear as a GP saying, “The record suggests this monitoring is overdue; let us check whether that is actually true and whether it matters today.” The patient does not need to interact directly with the software.

For the GP or practice team, the output may be a point-of-care prompt, medication alert, risk flag or population worklist. Primary Sense and Future Health Today are Australian examples. They are not necessarily generative AI, and that is part of the lesson: constrained tools doing a defined job may be more useful than open-ended generation.

### What the evidence says

Australian implementation work supports feasibility, acceptability and workflow evaluation. The evidence is much more specific than “decision support works”. Outcomes depend on the condition, rule, data quality, interface and how the practice responds. Some evaluations establish that a system can identify patients and be incorporated into care; that is not the same as proving improved patient outcomes in every practice.

### How this could apply in our practice

We may already have versions of this through our clinical software, PHN-supported tools or reports. A useful first step is to identify one high-value care gap—for example a defined monitoring or preventive-care task—and trace the whole process: how the patient is identified, who checks the record, who contacts the patient, what action is offered and how completion is recorded.

For Michael, after diabetes is identified, a governed prompt might surface missing monitoring or cardiovascular risk assessment. The GP opens the underlying data and rule, confirms whether the prompt is valid and discusses the action with him.

### What could stop it working

Incomplete coding, missing external results and stale records can generate false positives and false negatives. Too many low-value prompts create alert fatigue. A prompt that arrives at the wrong moment can compete with the patient’s reason for attending. A population report without a named owner is simply a list of unmet need.

We also need to be able to explain what the prompt is based on. The output should support judgement, not silently define the agenda.

**Take-home line:** The value is not the alert. The value is an accurate, actionable care gap with an owner.

## Slide 6 — The emerging second check

**Technology:** workflow-integrated generative clinical decision support; AI Consult is a research example

**Maturity:** emerging
**Time: 2½–3 minutes**

### What it is and how it works

This is closer to what many people imagine when they hear “clinical AI”. A model analyses structured and free-text information from the encounter and provides constrained diagnostic or treatment feedback inside the clinical workflow.

In the AI Consult research example, the system was integrated into a cloud medical record in Kenyan primary-care clinics. It analysed entered encounter data against instructions aligned to local guidance and returned graded feedback. Clinicians could accept, modify or ignore the suggestion.

For the patient, there may be no direct interface. The intended benefit is a second check before the plan is finalised. For the clinician, the safe sequence is independent assessment first, tool feedback second, verification and decision third. It should be a safety net, not the author of the diagnosis.

### What the evidence says

The 2026 pragmatic cluster-randomised trial included 9,691 patients. It did not show a statistically significant improvement in its primary outcome of 14-day treatment failure, although reviewed encounters showed better documentation quality. The setting, workforce, guidance and disease mix differ from Australian general practice, and the study was not powered for rare serious harms.

Separately, a randomised vignette study found that access to GPT-4 did not significantly improve physicians’ diagnostic-reasoning scores over conventional resources, despite strong model-alone performance. That illustrates a key evidence principle: the useful unit of evaluation is the **clinician–tool–workflow**, not the model by itself.

### How this could apply in our practice

At present, this is a category to understand and watch rather than a recommendation to adopt a public chatbot for patient-specific diagnosis or prescribing. If a governed Australian product became available, we would need to examine its intended purpose, ARTG status where applicable, supported population, local evidence, integration, failure handling and monitoring.

For Michael, a future system might flag that thirst and fatigue are not explained by BPH, or that the entered plan is incomplete. But we should not rewrite the story so the software “spots the diagnosis”.

### What could stop it working

A persuasive wrong suggestion may displace a sound clinical assessment. Silence may be mistaken for reassurance. Poorly entered facts produce confident but irrelevant output. Performance may not transfer across populations, disease prevalence, language, workflow or software versions.

If disagreement cannot be documented easily, or if clinicians cannot see the facts and intended purpose behind the output, the tool is not ready for safe use.

**Take-home line:** A capable model is not yet a safe clinical system. Integration, evidence, escalation and monitoring are part of the intervention.

## Slide 7 — Answer Michael’s question from sources

**Technology:** retrieval-augmented evidence assistant; MedLuma is the example

**Maturity:** current product; clinical benefit unestablished
**Time: 2½–3 minutes**

### What it is and how it works

Michael asks how well prostate artery embolisation works. “Works” could mean symptom improvement, avoiding surgery, adverse effects, durability or likelihood of needing another procedure. The first task is therefore to frame the question properly.

A retrieval-augmented evidence assistant searches a curated collection of guidelines and literature, retrieves passages relevant to the question, and uses a language model to produce a synthesis with links to the sources.

For the GP, this can accelerate source discovery. The safe workflow is: frame the question; read the synthesis; open the cited source; check that the source actually supports the claim; examine the population, comparator, outcomes and follow-up; and then discuss the evidence in the context of Michael’s priorities.

For Michael, the benefit is not receiving the generated paragraph. It is having a better-informed shared-decision conversation.

### What the evidence says

MedLuma is an Australian example that states it uses a curated clinical knowledge base and provides links to original sources. In this rapid review, no independent peer-reviewed evaluation of this product’s retrieval completeness, accuracy, clinical utility or patient outcomes was identified.

Citations are a useful design feature, but they are not a guarantee. A system can omit an important source, cite a document that supports only part of a sentence, flatten genuine disagreement or rely on an outdated version.

### How this could apply in our practice

This is suitable for a controlled team demonstration using a general, non-identifiable question. We could compare the answer with our normal evidence-search process and assess whether it finds the key Australian source, represents it accurately and makes uncertainty visible.

It may be useful for unfamiliar questions, guideline navigation or preparing a shared-decision discussion. It should not become a shortcut around reading the source.

### What could stop it working

The biggest adoption trap is citation theatre: the answer looks trustworthy because references appear beneath it. Another is copying a synthesis into the record or patient information without checking it. Identifiable patient information should not be entered into a public general-purpose tool; each product’s data handling still needs scrutiny.

I am deliberately not giving an efficacy number for prostate artery embolisation here because that clinical question has not been separately researched for this presentation.

**Take-home line:** Use the assistant to find evidence; do not confuse the assistant’s answer with the evidence.

## Slide 8 — Explain the plan in a usable form

**Technology:** source-grounded drafting and health-literacy adaptation

**Maturity:** current drafting capability; bounded clinical use
**Time: 2½–3 minutes**

### What it is and how it works

For the clinician, the process begins with approved facts and authoritative sources. We define the audience, purpose, reading level, format and required action. A language model drafts or rewrites the material. Then a human checks every claim and qualifier, reviews readability and accessibility, obtains consumer input where appropriate, and approves the final version.

For the patient, the output might be a one-page plan, a question prompt, large-print instructions or a clearer explanation of what happens next. More advanced personalisation could respond to a person’s stated preferences, but it should not infer literacy from age, postcode, diagnosis or education.

For Michael, the useful output is concise: what we found, what he needs to do this week, when to seek help and when follow-up will occur. The GP then uses teach-back. We do not assume that a clear-looking page has produced understanding.

### What the evidence says

Systematic and scoping reviews show promise for readability and user satisfaction, but accuracy, completeness and actionability are mixed. Much of the research is in English, and readability scores do not establish comprehension, cultural safety, informed choice or behaviour change.

The safest conclusion is that AI can help with drafting and alternative phrasing. It has not removed the need for clinical review, accessibility review or consumer testing.

### How this could apply in our practice

We could choose one common, low-risk information need and create a controlled template workflow: approved source material in; fixed required content and safety-netting; draft; clinical check; consumer or staff review; version control; and an owner for future updates.

This may also help generate several formats from the same approved content, such as a short after-visit summary and a longer information sheet.

### What could stop it working

Simplifying text may remove the very qualifier that keeps it safe. A polished document may contain an invented fact, ambiguous action or outdated link. Producing more material can create an update burden. If language support is needed for complex clinical communication or consent, a professional interpreter remains the current standard; a public translation tool is not a substitute.

**Take-home line:** The useful technology is not automatic writing. It is a controlled editorial workflow ending in a clinician-approved, patient-tested message.

## Slide 9 — Do not lose Michael to follow-up

**Technology:** prevention reports, reminders and recalls; future two-way outreach

**Maturity:** current identification; emerging autonomous action
**Time: 2½–3 minutes**

### What it is and how it works

Much of this technology is already present and is not necessarily AI. Current systems apply eligibility rules to structured records and produce a practice worklist. Clinical software, Primary Sense and registry integrations can help identify people who appear due for preventive care or monitoring.

For the patient, that becomes a reminder, a clinically important recall or a discussion when they next attend. These are different things. A reminder offers preventive care; a recall is proactive follow-up where attendance matters; a prompt draws attention during another episode of care.

For the practice, the work begins after the list is generated: check eligibility and contact details, choose an appropriate channel, send the communication, handle replies and non-response, escalate clinically significant information and document the outcome.

The emerging step is semi-autonomous two-way outreach: tailoring a message, answering simple questions, offering appointments and escalating exceptions.

### What the evidence says

Rules-based patient identification and reminder systems are current. The evidence and governance for safe semi-autonomous conversation and closed-loop action are much less mature. Automation of a message is not automation of responsibility.

### How this could apply in our practice

We could audit one existing recall pathway from start to finish. For Michael, we might confirm his preferred contact method—SMS with a phone fallback—check that he can correct the record, and make it easy to decline an optional preventive activity without being repeatedly chased.

If we later trial tailored automated outreach, the pilot should begin with low-risk communication and explicit escalation rules, not clinically complex replies.

### What could stop it working

Risks include contacting the wrong person, using incorrect eligibility data, repeating messages coercively, excluding people without reliable digital access, and receiving a concerning reply that nobody sees. A system may create apparent efficiency while shifting unrecognised work to reception or nursing staff.

Every worklist and incoming channel needs a named owner, response timeframe, exception process and fail-safe recall pathway.

**Take-home line:** Finding the patient is only the first step. Safe follow-up requires ownership all the way to closure.

## Slide 10 — Notice meaningful change

**Technology:** remote monitoring with longitudinal trend detection

**Maturity:** current monitoring infrastructure; emerging AI layer
**Time: 2½–3 minutes**

### What it is and how it works

For the patient, remote monitoring may involve taking home measurements or completing symptom reports with a connected device or app. The data are transmitted to a service or care team.

For the clinician, current systems present measurements and may apply simple thresholds. An emerging AI layer could combine repeated measurements with symptoms, medicines, pathology and a person’s baseline to prioritise meaningful change rather than showing a stream of raw numbers.

For Michael, a defined period of monitoring might reduce travel after a new diagnosis. The care plan must state what he measures, how often, who reviews it, what triggers contact, what happens after hours and what he should do when unwell or disconnected.

### What the evidence says

Remote-monitoring programs already exist in Australia, including programs using loan devices and apps. The existence of these services establishes that the infrastructure and care model can be current. It does not establish that an AI prediction layer is reliable, transferable or ready for autonomous response.

### How this could apply in our practice

This only helps if it addresses a defined clinical and access problem and if the practice has capacity to respond. A small, time-limited pathway with clear inclusion criteria is more credible than open-ended collection of data.

Before collecting the first measurement, we would need to identify the reviewing clinician or team, review frequency, thresholds, escalation, documentation, device support and the end point of monitoring.

### What could stop it working

False alarms can create workload and anxiety. Missing data may be mistaken for stability. Devices, connectivity, digital confidence and cost can widen inequity. Unclear after-hours responsibility can delay care. The most dangerous misunderstanding is that somebody is watching continuously when they are not.

**Take-home line:** Do not collect data until responsibility and response capacity are designed.

## Slide 11 — Choose more precisely—decide together

**Technology:** individual treatment-benefit and harm modelling

**Maturity:** research horizon
**Time: 2½–3 minutes**

### What it is and how it works

Most risk models estimate prognosis: what is likely to happen. Treatment-effect models attempt a harder question: how might the difference between two reasonable treatment options vary for this particular person?

The system combines relevant clinical features, and potentially biomarkers or longitudinal response, to estimate benefit and harm under different options. A useful interface would show uncertainty and make trade-offs visible.

For the clinician, this could support a more individualised discussion when several guideline-supported choices exist. For the patient, the output should create a better conversation, not a machine-selected prescription.

For Michael, a future diabetes-specific tool might compare reasonable options using comorbidities, medicines, renal function, longitudinal response and his priorities. Michael and the GP would still decide together.

### What the evidence says

The Australian PERSONAL trial, which is prospectively evaluating AI-supported antiseizure medicine selection, makes this a credible research direction. It is not evidence that a general-purpose treatment-optimisation system is available for Australian general practice or for Michael’s diabetes care.

Prediction of individual treatment effects is methodologically difficult. It requires representative data, external validation, calibration, prospective evaluation and evidence that using the model improves decisions or outcomes.

### How this could apply in our practice

There is no immediate adoption proposal here. The practical value is knowing what evidence to demand when products begin to make personalised treatment claims. We should ask whether the tool predicts prognosis or genuine treatment differences, whether uncertainty is shown, whether the relevant population was represented and whether the system has been tested in a real decision workflow.

### What could stop it working

False precision is the central danger. Biased or incomplete data can reproduce inequity. Recommendations can become obsolete as guidelines, medicines or circumstances change. Patient preferences may be reduced to input variables when they are actually choices to be explored.

**Take-home line:** More personalised prediction should strengthen shared decision-making, not disguise uncertainty or replace preference.

## Slide 12 — Bring retinal screening closer

**Technology:** automated image acquisition and task-specific classification

**Maturity:** emerging Australian implementation
**Time: 2½–3 minutes**

### What it is and how it works

This is narrow AI. A retinal camera captures an image. A model trained for a defined purpose analyses the image and estimates whether features of a particular referable condition are present. The output then enters a pathway for image quality, confirmatory assessment and referral.

For the patient, screening may occur closer to general practice, potentially reducing travel and missed screening. An ungradable image is not a negative result; it must trigger another pathway.

For the GP or practice team, implementation includes operating or supporting the camera, checking image quality, communicating the result, arranging confirmatory assessment and ensuring referral capacity. A classifier for one condition does not rule out every form of eye disease.

### What the evidence says

In a prospective Australian glaucoma-screening study in two general practices, 414 adults were recruited and 277 produced analysable images. In that real-world workflow, sensitivity was 65.0% and specificity 94.6%; image acquisition was a major limitation. Australian research has also examined AI-assisted diabetic-retinopathy screening in other clinical and Aboriginal Medical Service settings.

The important point is not to transfer the glaucoma performance numbers to diabetic-retinopathy screening. The evidence is task-, device-, population- and pathway-specific.

### How this could apply in our practice

For Michael, local retinal imaging after diabetes is identified could reduce the friction of completing recommended eye screening—but only if a suitable service and pathway exist. Before considering it, we would need to confirm the exact condition screened, intended purpose, regulatory status, equipment and training, reimbursement, consent, local referral options and what happens when an image is ungradable or discordant.

### What could stop it working

Ungradable images, false reassurance, equipment cost, operator training, referral bottlenecks and performance differences across groups can all undermine benefit. Bringing screening closer is not useful if abnormal or uncertain results cannot move into timely assessment.

**Take-home line:** Narrow AI can do one task well enough to help only when the acquisition, confirmation and referral pathway works around it.

## Slide 13 — Prepare better; do not outsource understanding

**Technologies:** diagnostic brainstorming and AI-assisted Anki

**Maturity:** exploratory or education-only
**Time: 3 minutes; optional on the short route**

These last two uses help the clinician prepare rather than directly treating Michael.

### Diagnostic brainstorming

The clinician gives a fictional or appropriately de-identified clinical representation to a language model and asks for possible diagnoses, discriminating findings or missing questions.

The safer sequence is **independent reasoning first, synthetic challenge second, authoritative verification third**. For Michael, the learner first recognises that thirst and fatigue do not fit neatly under BPH. Only then do they compare their differential with a synthetic response and ask what it added, omitted or over-weighted.

The evidence does not support treating a public chatbot as validated patient-specific decision support. In the randomised vignette trial mentioned earlier, LLM access did not significantly improve physician reasoning over conventional resources. Task-specific research systems may perform better in controlled studies, but that does not establish routine safety in our workflow.

The possible benefit is cognitive forcing: surfacing an option we did not consider. The pitfalls are anchoring, rare-disease noise, invented facts, unnecessary tests, privacy breaches and a fluent rationale that feels more reliable than it is.

In our practice, this is most defensible as an education activity using synthetic cases—not as an undocumented second opinion on an identifiable patient.

### AI-assisted Anki

Here, a model transforms already selected learning material into draft retrieval questions or cloze cards. The doctor checks that each card tests one clear point, preserves qualifiers and has a traceable source. Anki then schedules later review according to performance; the spaced-repetition function comes from Anki, not from the language model.

The evidence supports feasibility of AI-assisted resource generation, not a claim that AI-authored cards improve competence or patient outcomes. Faster production can generate a large deck of plausible clutter or encode errors that are repeatedly rehearsed.

A useful practice application would be a small shared deck from an agreed guideline update, with named clinical review and no patient-identifiable details. The barrier is quality control: every card saved today creates future review work.

**Take-home line:** Use AI to challenge and draft learning; never let it replace source selection, reasoning or verification.

## Slide 14 — Useful technology leaves care more human

**Time: 1½–2 minutes**

Across the journey, the systems perform different jobs. They may capture, retrieve, classify, prioritise, draft and monitor. Those verbs are specific, which makes them easier to evaluate than the single label “AI”.

Michael and the clinician still choose, interpret, verify, decide, respond and remain accountable.

If we wanted a practical starting point for our practice, I would suggest three conversations rather than a shopping list:

1. If we use or trial a scribe, can we standardise consent and audit the errors that matter?
2. Which existing care-gap or recall workflow has a clearly defined patient benefit but currently lacks reliable ownership or closure?
3. Is there one common patient-information task where a source-grounded, reviewed drafting workflow could improve usability without introducing unmanaged content?

For any proposed technology, we can use the same six questions:

- What exact task does this version perform?
- Is it current, emerging or a research horizon?
- Does the evidence test the clinician–tool workflow, not just the model?
- What patient data enter, leave, persist or train the system?
- Can the patient refuse and still receive ordinary care?
- What happens when the system is wrong, silent or unavailable?

The final message is this:

> Define the task. Appraise the workflow. Protect choice and data. Keep a fallback. Own the consequence.

[Pause.]

Thank you. I would be interested to hear which of these technologies feels most useful in our practice—and which hidden workload or risk we would want solved before adopting it.
