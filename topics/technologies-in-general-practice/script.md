# Technologies in General Practice — Presentation Script

> **Status:** teaching draft aligned to the patient-journey architecture on 12 July 2026 (Australia). Clinical, legal/privacy, consumer and Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander review remain required. This is education, not individual clinical, legal, privacy, procurement or cyber-security advice.

## Audience and format

- Australian GPs and GP registrars; senior medical students are a secondary audience.
- Approximately 40 minutes, with a shorter route available by omitting the clinician-learning appendix.
- Full-viewport presentation with visible fullscreen, keyboard navigation and progress.
- Named products illustrate categories without endorsement or comparative recommendation.

## Narrative question

> When does technology genuinely help the patient—and what must remain true for that help to be safe?

The presentation follows one fictional patient across preparation, consultation, follow-up and possible future care. It begins with Michael’s life and priorities rather than with a catalogue of tools.

## Learning outcomes

Learners should be able to:

1. distinguish current, emerging and research-horizon technologies in general practice;
2. explain how each technology changes the clinician–patient workflow;
3. identify plausible patient benefit without converting plausibility into evidence;
4. anticipate failure, data, access and equity risks;
5. state what clinician oversight and non-digital fallback are required; and
6. appraise a defined task, version, population and workflow rather than “AI” in the abstract.

## Michael — redesigned continuous case

Michael is a fictional 64-year-old self-employed school-bus driver who lives outside town. He has known BPH and books for worsening urinary frequency and nocturia. A pre-consultation account also surfaces thirst, fatigue, disrupted sleep and concern about remaining alert on his morning route. Ordinary clinical assessment and investigation later identify type 2 diabetes; no AI makes the diagnosis.

Michael:

- wants a concise written plan followed by teach-back;
- has patchy connectivity and cannot easily leave a school route for repeated appointments;
- asks how well prostate artery embolisation works, creating an evidence-retrieval and shared-decision task;
- may benefit from local retinal screening and defined remote monitoring after diabetes is recognised; and
- can refuse any technology, correct the record and choose a phone, paper or ordinary-consultation pathway.

Do not add exact diabetes results, treatment advice or a numerical prostate artery embolisation answer without separate clinical research and review.

## Scene sequence and presenter notes

### 1. Technology should help the patient (2 minutes)

Open with the patient-centred test: impressive output is not the outcome. Ask the audience to notice where each system creates new work, uncertainty or dependence.

### 2. Meet Michael (3 minutes)

Introduce Michael’s work, travel and communication preferences before his diagnoses. Establish that “urinary symptoms” is only the booking label. The ordinary consultation—not an AI system—recognises that thirst and fatigue need wider assessment.

### 3. Before the consultation: tell the whole story (3 minutes)

Use BetterConsult only as an example of adaptive pre-consultation history. The benefit is agenda discovery, not diagnosis. Emphasise that incomplete forms, disability, limited English, literacy or connectivity must not block access. A normal consultation remains available.

### 4. During the consultation: preserve attention and the record (4 minutes)

Describe the ambient-scribe sequence: explain, consent, consult, stop, review, correct and authorise. Lyrebird may be named once as a personal example, not an endorsement. Ask what happens if the draft invents an examination or omits Michael’s route-safety concern.

### 5. During the consultation: surface a defined care gap (3 minutes)

Contrast governed, evidence-linked prompts with open-ended generation. Primary Sense and Future Health Today illustrate structured data, rules and worklists. The GP checks the underlying data and decides whether a prompt applies; alert fatigue and missing external results remain visible.

### 6. During the consultation: the emerging second check (3 minutes)

Use AI Consult as research evidence, not an Australian product recommendation. Explain why integration, constraint and prospective workflow evaluation matter. Its 2026 Kenyan trial is an implementation milestone, not proof of Australian transferability or improved patient outcomes.

### 7. After the consultation: answer from sources (3 minutes)

Michael asks how well prostate artery embolisation works. MedLuma illustrates retrieval with citations. Demonstrate the sequence: define “works”, inspect population and comparator, open the source, examine follow-up and outcomes, then return to Michael’s priorities. Do not display an unresearched efficacy number.

### 8. After the consultation: make the plan usable (3 minutes)

Show the controlled editorial workflow: authoritative source, defined audience and action, draft, claim check, readability/accessibility review, consumer review, clinician approval and teach-back. If language support is needed, a professional interpreter remains the current clinical standard for complex communication and consent.

### 9. Between consultations: do not lose follow-up (3 minutes)

Separate current rules-based identification from future semi-autonomous two-way outreach. Michael chooses how he is contacted and can correct or decline. A concerning reply, non-response or uncertain eligibility must reach a person through a defined fail-safe process.

### 10. Between consultations: notice meaningful change (3 minutes)

Remote monitoring is current; reliable AI prioritisation and closed-loop response are emerging. State who reviews data, when, what triggers contact, what happens after hours and what Michael should do when unwell or disconnected. Never imply continuous watching.

### 11. Future treatment: choose more precisely, decide together (3 minutes)

Use the PERSONAL trial to ground treatment-effect modelling as a research horizon. A future diabetes tool might compare reasonable options and show uncertainty; it does not replace shared decision-making or establish that a general prescribing engine exists.

### 12. Narrow image AI: retinal screening (3 minutes)

Use Australian general-practice research to show a narrow classifier inside an acquisition and referral pathway. Discuss ungradable images, confirmatory assessment, equipment, local deployment and referral capacity. One classifier does not rule out all eye disease.

### 13. Clinician-learning appendix (optional, 4 minutes)

Keep this subordinate to Michael’s care. For differential brainstorming: independent reasoning first, synthetic challenge second, authoritative verification third. For Anki: selected sources, one testable point, qualifiers and deletion of plausible clutter. No identifiable patient information enters a public tool.

### 14. Close: useful technology leaves care more human (2 minutes)

Return to the shared boundary:

> Systems may capture, retrieve, classify, prioritise, draft and monitor. Patients and clinicians still choose, interpret, verify, decide, respond and remain accountable.

Final transfer line:

> Define the task. Appraise the workflow. Protect choice and data. Keep a fallback. Own the consequence.

## Demonstration and content boundaries

- Use only fictional, synthetic or properly de-identified material.
- Product pages describe functionality only and are not evidence of benefit.
- Do not endorse or rank commercial products.
- Do not enter identifiable or sensitive information into a public generative-AI service.
- Do not present current reminders, recalls or remote monitoring as inherently AI.
- Do not present machine translation as a substitute for a professional interpreter in complex clinical communication or consent.
- Do not promise time saving, diagnostic improvement, burnout reduction, cost reduction or autonomous treatment optimisation.
- Display “Australia · current as at 12 July 2026” wherever regulatory content appears.
