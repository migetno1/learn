# Technologies in General Practice — supporting AI research

> Research status: populated rapid evidence review for teaching development. Current as at 12 July 2026 (Australia). Clinical, legal/privacy, consumer and Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander review are still required before publication or presentation. This file is educational research, not individual clinical, legal, privacy, procurement or cyber-security advice.

## Research scope and method

- **Primary audience:** GP registrars and practising GPs; senior medical students are a secondary audience.
- **Primary setting:** Australian general practice, including rural and regional practice.
- **Core question:** How is AI already assisting a clinician’s work across care and education, what may come next, and which responsibilities remain with the clinician?
- **Clinical focus:** ambient scribes, educational brainstorming, evidence retrieval, patient-information drafting, medical-education content and selected emerging clinical applications.
- **System focus:** evidence, intended purpose, accountability, transparency, consent, privacy, records, human factors, equity, implementation and monitoring.
- **Jurisdiction:** Australia. State and territory surveillance, privacy and health-record law is flagged but not exhaustively mapped in this rapid review.

This was a targeted rapid review, not a systematic review. Searches on 12 July 2026 covered current Australian regulator, safety, privacy and professional guidance, followed by recent systematic reviews, randomised studies and major evaluation studies for the selected use cases. Sources were prioritised for authority, clinical relevance and methodological strength. Product comparisons, vendor claims and preprints were not used to establish teaching claims.

“AI in medicine” is too broad for one useful lesson. The revised teaching direction follows **one fictional patient across a clinician’s working day**, using the presenter’s current workflows as concrete examples and a short three-horizon view of possible future uses. The TASK appraisal framework remains a recurring safety lens rather than the narrative structure. This keeps the topic practical without implying that every AI system has the same evidence, risk or regulatory status.

## Findings

### 1. Start with the task, not the label “AI”

**Established regulatory position and authoritative guidance**

- Australian medical-device regulation is technology-neutral. Whether software is a medical device depends primarily on its intended purpose, not merely on whether it uses AI. Software intended for diagnosis, prevention, monitoring, prediction, prognosis or treatment may meet the statutory definition and generally must be included in the ARTG before lawful supply unless an exclusion or exemption applies. [S3–S5]
- A general-purpose ambient scribe that records and summarises a consultation will usually not have a therapeutic intended purpose. If it adds diagnoses, investigations or treatments not stated in the consultation, its intended purpose and regulatory status may change. [S1–S3, S7]
- ARTG inclusion is not a judgement that the tool is appropriate for every patient, population, workflow or off-label use. The practitioner still has to understand the intended purpose, follow instructions, assess suitability and apply professional judgement. [S1–S5]

**Teaching implication**

Ask five questions before discussing whether “AI works”:

1. What exact task does this version perform?
2. Who acts on its output?
3. What patient data does it receive, generate, retain or disclose?
4. What happens if it is wrong, silent or unavailable?
5. Is this use inside the manufacturer’s intended purpose and local governance?

Do not transfer evidence between transcription, summarisation, prediction, diagnosis and treatment recommendation merely because all are described as AI.

### 2. Clinician accountability does not transfer to the tool

**Authoritative professional guidance**

- Ahpra and National Boards state that practitioners remain responsible for safe, quality care and must apply human judgement to AI output. TGA status does not remove that responsibility. [S2]
- Practitioners should understand enough about the tool to use it safely: intended use, populations used for training and testing, limitations, unsuitable contexts, data location, storage and any use of data for retraining. [S2]
- Records, results and other outputs that may be acted on require timely review for accuracy, completeness and relevance. Errors must be corrected before clinician-authorised information is saved or relied on. [S1, S2, S4, S7]
- AI involvement should be transparent. ACSQHC recommends labelling records where AI contributed to their creation, following organisational procedures. [S1]

**Practical interpretation**

“Human in the loop” is not satisfied by a ceremonial click. Review must be feasible, timely and matched to risk. For a scribe this includes checking patient identity, medicines, allergies, red flags, examination findings, chronology, assessment, plan, follow-up and anything carried into referrals or billing.

### 3. What counts as useful evidence

**Empirical finding**

- The 2024 JAMA systematic review found that most LLM healthcare evaluations were benchmark or question-answering studies; only 5% of 519 studies used real patient-care data. Accuracy dominated, while fairness, bias and toxicity were less often assessed. [S13]
- Early-stage clinical evaluation should include the human–computer interaction and the workflow, not only model performance. Reporting frameworks such as DECIDE-AI, CONSORT-AI, SPIRIT-AI, TRIPOD+AI and STARD-AI help identify whether important details are reported. [S20]

**Appraisal ladder for teaching**

1. **Technical performance:** does the model perform the computational task on an appropriate test set?
2. **External validity:** does it perform in a separate population, site and workflow?
3. **Human–AI performance:** do intended users employ it safely, and how does it change decisions and workload?
4. **Clinical utility:** does it improve care compared with the real alternative?
5. **Patient and system outcomes:** are benefits, harms, equity, cost and unintended consequences measured over time?

Licensing-exam performance, a polished demo or a favourable standalone accuracy score cannot establish clinical utility.

Minimum appraisal questions include the model and version, intended purpose, comparator, reference standard, study population, exclusions, missing data, calibration, subgroup performance, workflow, overrides, patient outcomes, adverse events, funding and conflicts. A software update may make an earlier evaluation less applicable.

### 4. Ambient scribes: promising workflow evidence, incomplete safety evidence

**Empirical findings**

- The 2025 *eBioMedicine* systematic review included nine heterogeneous studies involving 524 healthcare professionals, 616 patients and 1,069 consultations. Studies reporting effectiveness, patient-centredness or efficiency were generally favourable, but safety was inconclusive in the six studies that assessed it; three raised safety concerns. Seven studies were from the US, with none from Australia. [S9]
- A separate 2025 systematic review of 11 implementation studies found that nine of ten studies reporting efficiency showed improvement in at least one measure and seven of ten reported favourable clinician-wellness findings. Accuracy varied, manual edits were frequently needed and patient experience was assessed in only three studies. [S10]
- In a pragmatic US randomised trial of 238 outpatient physicians across 14 specialties, one of two scribes produced a modest reduction in time in notes relative to usual care; the other did not show the same primary-outcome effect. Both had favourable secondary signals for burnout, work exhaustion or task load. The authors called for longer-term, multisite, patient-safety and cost-effectiveness work. [S11]
- A US multicentre before-and-after quality-improvement study reported lower self-reported burnout after 30 days and improvements in cognitive task load and after-hours documentation. It had no control group, voluntary recruitment, possible response and early-adopter bias, subjective outcomes and vendor involvement in data collection. It supports a signal, not a causal promise. [S12]

**What is established enough to teach**

- Some clinicians in studied outpatient settings experience modest time or cognitive-load benefits.
- Benefit varies by product, clinician, task and workflow; it is not safe to promise time savings or burnout reduction to an individual GP or practice.
- Draft notes require active review. Known failure modes include omission, fabrication, mishearing, incorrect chronology or categorisation, and failure to capture non-verbal information, examination findings or relevant data outside the spoken encounter. [S1, S2, S7, S9, S10]
- Evidence for downstream patient outcomes, rare harms, equity, Australian general practice, cost-effectiveness and long-term skill effects remains limited.

### 5. Diagnostic support: tool performance is not team performance

**Empirical findings**

- In a randomised study of 50 US physicians solving six clinical vignettes, access to GPT-4 plus conventional resources did not significantly improve diagnostic-reasoning scores compared with conventional resources alone: median 76% versus 74%, adjusted difference 2 percentage points (95% CI −4 to 8). The model alone scored higher in an exploratory analysis, but this did not translate into improved physician–AI performance. [S14]
- Other decision-support studies are task-specific. A positive result for one imaging, prediction or treatment task cannot be generalised to open-ended general practice, a different prevalence, or a different model version. [S13, S17, S20]

**Interpretation**

The relevant unit of evaluation is often the **clinician–tool–workflow**, not the model alone. Access without training, interface design, reliable input data, escalation and monitoring may produce little benefit or new failure modes. If an output conflicts with the patient’s presentation or a reliable source, pause, verify the underlying facts and use ordinary clinical escalation rather than averaging the two answers.

### 6. Patient-facing general-purpose chatbots: engage without endorsing

**Empirical findings**

- In a preregistered study of 1,298 UK participants across ten medical scenarios, tested LLMs performed strongly when run alone but participants using them did no better than controls at identifying conditions or choosing disposition. The study demonstrates that benchmark performance does not predict real user benefit. [S15]
- A 2026 physician-led red-team study assessed 888 responses to 222 primary-care questions. Depending on the tested chatbot, 21.6%–43.2% of responses were rated problematic and 5%–13% unsafe. These are model- and version-specific simulated results, not fixed properties or population incidence estimates. [S16]

**Suggested GP response — teaching interpretation**

1. Ask what the patient asked, what the system answered and what action they are considering.
2. Acknowledge the useful parts without validating the output as a whole.
3. Reassess urgency using the history, examination and reliable clinical sources.
4. Identify missing context, incorrect claims, unsafe disposition or medicine advice.
5. Explain uncertainty and agree on a safe next step.

Avoid shaming the patient; it may suppress disclosure. Do not upload their information to a public chatbot to “check” the answer.

### 7. Automation bias and generative failure modes

**Authoritative guidance and empirical synthesis**

- ACSQHC defines automation bias as over-relying on the tool over independent judgement. It can produce errors of commission—acting on a wrong recommendation—and errors of omission—failing to act when the system misses something. [S1]
- Generative systems can produce fluent, internally coherent text that is false, unsupported or incomplete. Confidence of wording is not calibrated clinical confidence. [S1, S13–S16]
- Bias can enter at data collection, feature and label construction, model development, evaluation, deployment and publication. Performance may deteriorate outside the development cohort and may do so differently across subgroups. [S17]

**Safeguards — teaching interpretation**

- Form an initial assessment before viewing high-stakes AI advice where feasible.
- Verify critical facts against the original history, examination, investigation or authoritative source.
- Deliberately search for what the output omitted, not only what it got wrong.
- Make disagreement and override easy to record and review.
- Monitor false positives, false negatives, overrides, corrections, incidents and subgroup outcomes.
- Preserve a manual fallback and avoid eroding core documentation and reasoning skills in learners.

### 8. Transparency, consent, privacy and recording

**Authoritative guidance**

- Ahpra advises informing patients about AI use and involving them in the decision where personal data is required. It states that generative ambient scribing generally requires informed consent and that the response should ideally be recorded. [S2]
- ACSQHC recommends proportionate disclosure and consent based on purpose, risk, privacy, data use and third-party sharing. Patients should understand why the tool is used, its benefits and limitations, that the clinician reviews the output and what alternative is available. [S1]
- The Privacy Act and Australian Privacy Principles apply to covered organisations using AI with personal information. OAIC recommends privacy-by-design, a privacy impact assessment for higher-risk uses, due diligence, clear policies and ongoing review. [S6]
- OAIC recommends not entering personal, particularly sensitive, information into publicly available generative AI tools because of significant and complex privacy risks. [S6]
- Recording laws differ across states and territories and may create criminal risk. RACGP advises GPs to check local surveillance-device law and seek MDO or legal advice. This rapid review does not provide a jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction legal conclusion. [S2, S7]

**Consent content for an ambient scribe**

Explain in plain language:

- that the consultation will be captured for a draft note;
- what is captured, whether audio or transcript is retained and for how long;
- who processes, stores or can access the information and in which countries;
- whether data can be used to train systems or for another secondary purpose;
- that the GP checks and authorises the final record;
- that saying no will not reduce ordinary care and what documentation alternative will be used.

Do not make consent a rushed click after recording has begun. Follow current practice policy, state or territory law and MDO advice about verbal versus written consent and frequency of renewal.

### 9. Equity, cultural safety and Indigenous data governance

**Empirical and community-led findings**

- Under-representation, historically biased labels, missing social context and deployment choices can produce different performance or benefit across groups. Overall accuracy can hide clinically important subgroup harm. [S1, S8, S17]
- The 2025 CSIRO scoping project, co-led with Indigenous partner organisations and involving 53 participants across four workshops, identified three priorities: AI health literacy and cultural appropriateness; protection of Indigenous data sovereignty under Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander custodianship; and self-determination through Indigenous organisations leading design, implementation and oversight. [S18]
- Indigenous data sovereignty concerns collective authority and governance, not merely individual privacy or de-identification. [S18, S19]

**Implementation implication**

An Australian practice or health service should not infer cultural safety from generic vendor claims. Evaluation should include local languages, accents, communication styles, disability access, multimorbidity, rural connectivity and relevant subgroup outcomes. For systems using Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander data or intended for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities, engage Aboriginal Community Controlled Health Organisations and relevant communities early, with genuine authority over purpose, data and oversight.

### 10. Practice implementation across the lifecycle

**Before adoption** [S1–S8]

1. Define the clinical or operational problem and a non-AI comparator.
2. Identify intended purpose, version, user, workflow and failure consequences.
3. Check ARTG status where the use may meet the medical-device definition; read instructions and limitations.
4. Appraise evidence for the local population and real workflow, not only a benchmark.
5. Map every data flow: collection, transcription, storage, location, access, subcontractors, retention, deletion, training and secondary use.
6. Complete privacy, security, clinical-safety, equity and contractual review; seek legal or MDO input where needed.
7. Define consent, refusal, manual fallback, incident response and stop criteria.
8. Pilot locally with trained users and representative cases before scale-up.

**During use** [S1, S2, S4, S7]

1. Confirm the patient and consent before activation where required.
2. Use only for the approved purpose and population.
3. Keep the patient—not the interface—as the primary source of clinical context.
4. Review critical output promptly and correct it before authorisation or downstream use.
5. Record material actions, disagreement, override and follow-up.
6. Use the manual fallback if quality, connectivity or workflow makes review unsafe.

**After implementation** [S1, S3, S6–S8]

1. Monitor adoption and non-use as well as time, corrections, omissions, incidents, complaints and patient refusal.
2. Examine performance and benefit by relevant population and workflow subgroups.
3. Review privacy, security, vendor and model changes; do not “set and forget”.
4. Reassess intended purpose and evidence after material updates or feature creep.
5. Report medical-device adverse events to the TGA and privacy or clinical incidents through applicable channels.
6. Pause or retire the tool when harms outweigh benefits, review becomes infeasible or the evidence no longer matches the deployed version.

### 11. A compact clinical framework

The research supports the mnemonic **TASK**, as a teaching synthesis rather than an external standard:

- **T — Task:** What exact job is this version meant to do, and is this use within scope?
- **A — Appraise:** What evidence supports it for this patient group and workflow?
- **S — Share and safeguard:** Explain its role, obtain proportionate consent, protect data and keep a fallback.
- **K — Keep responsibility:** Check the output, act on the patient’s context, document and monitor.

### 12. Current use cases and the presenter's workflow

**Established current landscape**

The Australian Government describes current health-care uses across documentation, decision support, screening and image analysis, patient-support chatbots, surgery, medical-record risk analysis and wound monitoring. These are distinct tasks with different evidence, users and failure consequences; inclusion in a national overview is not proof of benefit for a particular product or workflow. [S21]

**Presenter-reported uses — experience, not comparative evidence**

- Ambient documentation with Lyrebird, with patient consent and clinician review before the note enters the record. Lyrebird may be named as the presenter's tool without endorsement or transfer of vendor claims. [S1, S2, S7, S9–S12, S23]
- Research and presentation development: question framing, outlining, source discovery and drafting, followed by checking the primary source and rewriting in the clinician-educator's own judgement. [S13, S20, S25]
- Drafting Anki cards from already selected learning material. AI can reduce the blank-page burden, but every card still needs checking for a single clear learning point, an accurate answer, appropriate context and a traceable source. [S25, S26]
- Brainstorming possible differential diagnoses and investigations for fictional, de-identified or explicitly educational cases. This is a learning prompt, not individual medical advice or autonomous decision support. Learners should form an initial view first, check suggestions against the case and authoritative sources, and ask what the list omitted. [S13, S14, S17, S25]
- Evidence retrieval with MedLuma. Its first-party description says answers link to original sources from a curated Australian knowledge base. Teaching should demonstrate opening and checking those sources rather than treating the generated synthesis as the evidence itself. [S20, S22]
- Drafting patient-information materials. Early evidence is promising but mixed across conditions, models and evaluation methods; clinician review, source checking, readability review and consumer review remain necessary. [S24]

**Future-use teaching boundary**

Potential future uses should be organised by horizon, not presented as predictions: extensions of current record synthesis and decision support; more personalised communication and longitudinal monitoring; and more autonomous coordination or treatment optimisation. Each step towards greater autonomy increases the need for prospective evidence, clear intended purpose, patient involvement, governance, monitoring and a usable fallback. [S1, S3–S5, S13, S17, S20, S21]

## Teaching interpretation separated from researched claims

- Follow the presenter and Michael, a fictional man with known BPH and symptoms that ultimately lead to a type 2 diabetes diagnosis, across the consultation and the presenter’s later educational work. The case connects documentation, educational brainstorming, evidence retrieval and patient-information drafting without implying that AI made the diagnosis.
- Use the ambient-scribe scene to expose consent, privacy, documentation, workflow and verification in a familiar consultation.
- Present educational differential brainstorming only after learners form an initial view, showing why model-alone performance is insufficient and why omission matters.
- Use Michael’s question about prostate artery embolisation to demonstrate evidence retrieval and source checking, not to teach an unresearched efficacy estimate.
- Use the future type 2 diabetes fact sheet as a visibly labelled placeholder until its clinical content and destination site have been reviewed.
- Teach the smallest amount of model theory needed to understand probabilistic output, omission, bias and drift.
- Avoid “AI versus doctor” framing. The clinically relevant question is whether a defined sociotechnical system improves care compared with the real alternative.
- Make patient refusal ordinary and safe in the vignette; the GP should have a credible non-AI workflow.
- Present **TASK** as a recurring memory aid developed for this lesson, not as a validated standard or the page’s main structure.
- Position AI literacy as clinical literacy: intended purpose, evidence, uncertainty, communication, privacy, equity and escalation.

## Open verification and review items

- Obtain legal/privacy review before teaching detailed consent, recording, cross-border disclosure or automated-decision provisions. Map state and territory surveillance-device law only if the final lesson needs jurisdiction-specific instructions.
- Recheck commencement and practical effect of Privacy Act automated-decision transparency reforms expected in December 2026; do not present future provisions as current law.
- Reopen the TGA, ACSQHC, Ahpra, OAIC and RACGP sources immediately before publication because each area is evolving.
- Check whether ACSQHC has replaced version 1.0 of the AI Clinical Use Guide.
- Confirm whether new Australian ambient-scribe trials or post-market safety data materially change the evidence summary.
- Seek a privacy officer or MDO view on the final patient-consent wording.
- Obtain review from an Aboriginal Community Controlled Health Organisation or appropriately authorised Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander reviewers. The current synthesis cannot substitute for community authority.
- Obtain consumer review, including people with disability, culturally and linguistically diverse patients and rural or remote patients.
- If any commercial product is named later, verify its current version, intended purpose, ARTG status, data flows and terms on the day of review. Prefer product-neutral teaching.
- Consider a formal systematic review only if the lesson will make comparative effectiveness claims or underpin procurement guidance.

## Excluded from public teaching content

- Patient-identifiable prompts, recordings, transcripts or screenshots.
- Commercial product rankings or endorsements.
- Legal conclusions that apply uniformly across every Australian jurisdiction.
- Unqualified claims that AI saves time, reduces burnout, improves diagnosis, lowers cost or reduces inequity.
- Model-specific percentages presented as permanent characteristics after the model or interface changes.
- Autonomous diagnosis or treatment advice presented as an acceptable substitute for clinical care.
- Vendor marketing used as evidence of benefit or safety.
