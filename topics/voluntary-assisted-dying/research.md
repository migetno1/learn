## Findings

### 1. Current legislative status in NSW

**Established legal facts**

- VAD has been a lawful end-of-life option for eligible adults in NSW since 28 November 2023 under the *Voluntary Assisted Dying Act 2022* (NSW). [S1]
- The Act remains in force in the current consolidation checked on 11 July 2026. [S1]
- VAD is not a nationally uniform process. Each participating state or territory has its own legislation, eligibility rules, practitioner duties and service model. Teaching should label all NSW-specific content as NSW content. [S9]
- The NSW Act treats a death following lawful administration of a VAD substance as not being suicide for NSW law. [S1, s 12]
- The Act’s first statutory review is underway, but a review paper or proposal does not itself change the law. [S12]

**Publishing implication**

Use: “In NSW, current as at 11 July 2026.” Do not say “the Australian VAD law” or imply that the NSW process applies nationally.

### 2. Eligibility and formal process

#### Eligibility: every criterion must be met

At the relevant assessment points, the person must: [S1, ss 6 and 16; S2]

- be aged 18 years or older;
- be an Australian citizen, a permanent resident, or have lived in Australia for at least three continuous years at the time of the first request;
- have been ordinarily resident in NSW for at least 12 months at the time of the first request, unless the Board grants a residency exemption on compassionate grounds to a person with a substantial connection to NSW;
- have at least one diagnosed disease, illness or medical condition that is advanced, progressive and will cause death;
- be expected, on the balance of probabilities, to die within six months, or within 12 months where the condition is neurodegenerative;
- be experiencing suffering caused by the condition that cannot be relieved in a way the person considers tolerable;
- have decision-making capacity in relation to VAD;
- be acting voluntarily and not because of pressure or duress; and
- have an enduring request for VAD.

A person is not eligible **merely because** they have disability, dementia or a mental health impairment. Those conditions are not automatic exclusions either: the person may be eligible only if every criterion is met, including the disease/prognosis criteria and VAD-specific decision-making capacity. [S1, s 16]

If the person permanently loses VAD-specific decision-making capacity during the request and assessment process, they cease to be eligible. No family member, guardian, enduring guardian or substitute decision-maker can request VAD for them. [S1; S2]

#### Practitioner eligibility and roles

- A coordinating or consulting practitioner must be a medical practitioner with specialist registration, or general registration plus at least 10 years’ practice while generally registered; must complete approved VAD training; and must meet the Act’s conflict-of-interest and other requirements. A suitably registered GP can therefore qualify, but GP status alone is not enough. [S1, s 18]
- The coordinating practitioner accepts the first request, performs the first assessment and coordinates the process. [S1; S2]
- A separate consulting practitioner independently performs the second assessment. [S1, s 36]
- An administering practitioner may be a qualifying medical practitioner or nurse practitioner who has completed approved training. A nurse practitioner can administer under the Act but cannot be the coordinating or consulting practitioner or prescribe the VAD substance. [S1, s 55; S11]

#### Request and assessment sequence

NSW Health’s patient-facing summary describes 11 steps: [S5]

1. The patient makes a first request to a medical practitioner.
2. A coordinating practitioner completes the first assessment.
3. A consulting practitioner independently completes the consulting assessment.
4. The patient completes a written declaration.
5. The patient makes a final request.
6. The coordinating practitioner completes a final review.
7. The patient makes an administration decision with the coordinating practitioner.
8. The coordinating practitioner applies to the Board for a substance authorisation and the Board decides the application.
9. The coordinating practitioner prescribes the substance after authorisation.
10. The patient self-administers or asks an administering practitioner to administer the substance, if and when the patient chooses.
11. Death certification and statutory notifications occur.

Important detail behind the summary: [S1; S2]

- The first request must be made by the patient, be clear and unambiguous, occur during a medical consultation, and be made in person or, where permitted, by audiovisual communication. It may be verbal or communicated another way, including gestures. A general enquiry is not necessarily a first request.
- The patient can pause or stop at any time and does not need to give a reason. Receiving the substance does not oblige the patient to use it.
- Both the coordinating and consulting practitioners must independently assess every eligibility criterion. If unable to decide particular clinical, capacity, voluntariness or duress matters, the Act requires referral to a person with the appropriate skills and training for an opinion.
- The written declaration follows two positive assessments, uses the approved form, and is signed by the patient (or a permitted person at the patient’s direction) in the presence of two eligible witnesses.
- The final request ordinarily cannot be made before the end of the designated period, which runs from the first-request day until the day five days later, and cannot be made until after the consulting assessment day. The practitioners may agree to an earlier final request only where the patient is likely to die or lose VAD-specific capacity before that period ends.
- At final review, the coordinating practitioner checks the documents and remains satisfied about capacity, voluntariness, absence of pressure or duress, and the enduring request.

#### Prescribing, supply and administration

- Only the coordinating practitioner may prescribe the VAD substance, and only after the request and assessment process, an administration decision, any required contact-person appointment, and Board substance authorisation. [S1, Parts 3–4]
- The prescription goes directly to the authorised supplier. In the NSW model, the NSW VAD Pharmacy Service coordinates procurement, supply and disposal; the prescription is not handed to the patient to take to a community pharmacy. [S1, s 74; S2; S3]
- The patient chooses self-administration or practitioner administration in consultation with the coordinating practitioner and may later revoke or change that decision in accordance with the Act. [S1, ss 57–58]
- Self-administration requires appointment of a consenting adult contact person, who has statutory responsibilities including returning unused or remaining substance within the required time. [S1, ss 65–68 and 125]
- Practitioner administration requires an eligible administering practitioner, the patient’s continuing eligibility and request at administration, and an eligible witness. [S1, ss 60–64]
- Substance-specific instructions belong to the restricted Prescription and Administration Handbook and authorised-practitioner training. They should not be reproduced in public teaching content. [S2]

#### Documentation and reporting

The Act creates a form-and-notification trail to the NSW VAD Board. Most practitioner reports are due within five business days. These include the first request decision, first and consulting assessments, written declaration, final request and review, administration decision or revocation, prescription, supply, administration or disposal, practitioner transfers, and notification of death as applicable. [S1]

For a GP receiving a first request, the immediately relevant requirements are:

- record the first request, the decision to accept or refuse, the reason for refusal if applicable, and whether the approved information was given; [S1, s 22]
- complete and submit the First Request Form to the Board within five business days after deciding to accept or refuse; [S1, s 23]
- if completing the Medical Certificate of Cause of Death for a person known or reasonably believed to have died following lawful VAD administration, include the information required by s 87 and submit the applicable death notification within five business days unless an exception applies. [S1, s 87; S4]

The public death certificate issued to family is not the same document as the medical cause-of-death notification. Avoid the oversimplification that “VAD is never recorded”: the Act requires specified VAD information in the medical cause-of-death certificate and requires the underlying qualifying disease, illness or condition to be identified. [S1, s 87]

### 3. What a GP may, must and must not do

#### A GP may

- listen, clarify what the patient is asking, answer patient-initiated questions within the GP’s competence and provide reliable information; [S4]
- initiate a VAD discussion in NSW **only** while also giving the information required by s 10 about standard treatment options, likely outcomes, palliative care options and their likely outcomes; [S1, s 10]
- accept a valid first request only if eligible and willing to become the coordinating practitioner; [S1, ss 18–24]
- decline to participate because of conscientious objection, other unwillingness, inability or ineligibility, subject to the response, documentation and reporting duties below; [S1, ss 9 and 21]
- continue all usual GP care, provide records or clinical information with consent and support palliative, psychosocial, cultural, spiritual and family care without becoming an authorised VAD practitioner; [S2; S4; S9]
- give the patient the local or statewide Care Navigator details or, with consent, help connect them to an authorised practitioner. [S4; S7; S8]

#### A GP must when a clear first request is made

- recognise that a clear and unambiguous request during a medical consultation may trigger the formal first-request duties even if the GP is not authorised or intends to object; [S1, s 19; S4]
- decide to accept or refuse. An ineligible practitioner must refuse; [S1, s 21]
- tell the patient the decision within two business days, except that refusal because of conscientious objection must be communicated immediately; [S1, s 21]
- give the current approved first-request information within two business days unless refusing because of conscientious objection, for which the Act does not impose that information-giving duty; [S4]
- make the required medical-record entry and submit the First Request Form to the Board within five business days of the decision, including when refusing and including when conscientiously objecting. [S1, ss 22–23; S4]

#### A GP must not

- treat a family member’s, carer’s or substitute decision-maker’s request as the patient’s first request; [S1, s 19; S2]
- provide information beyond their competence or present a preliminary view as a formal eligibility determination; [S4]
- initiate VAD as a stand-alone suggestion without the same-discussion treatment and palliative-care information required by s 10; [S1]
- pressure, coerce or induce a patient to request or continue VAD; [S1]
- withdraw services they would ordinarily provide to the patient or the patient’s family because the patient requested VAD; [S1, s 28(1)(k); S4]
- obstruct access to the patient’s records or lawful care because of personal objection; [S4; S10]
- prescribe or administer unless authorised, trained, eligible and acting within the relevant statutory role; [S1]
- send substance-specific administration advice over telephone, email, fax, videoconference or another carriage service without following current authorised guidance. The NSW handbook identifies unresolved Commonwealth Criminal Code risk even though NSW law states VAD is not suicide. [S2]

### 4. Conscientious objection, continuity and referral

Keep three layers distinct:

1. **Statutory right:** a registered health practitioner with a conscientious objection may refuse to participate in the request and assessment process, prescribe, supply or administer the substance, or be present at administration. [S1, s 9]
2. **Residual statutory duties:** a medical practitioner receiving a first request must still refuse immediately, document it and notify the Board. A conscientious objector is not legally required by the Act to provide the approved first-request information. [S1, ss 21–23; S4]
3. **Continuity and professional duties:** the Act prohibits a treating practitioner from withdrawing ordinary services because of the request. The Medical Board code requires a doctor to inform the patient of the objection and not use it to impede access to legal treatment or allow personal views to deny medical care. NSW Health says a practitioner unable to accept should refer the patient to the Care Navigator Service or an authorised practitioner as appropriate. [S1, s 28(1)(k); S4; S10]

**Conclusion for teaching:** NSW does not create an express statutory duty for an individual conscientious objector to make a named clinician-to-clinician VAD referral. However, “no statutory referral duty” is not permission to abandon or obstruct the patient. A practical, professionally safer response is to disclose the boundary respectfully, complete the legal first-request duties, reassure the patient that ordinary care continues, and offer the Care Navigator pathway or another authorised practitioner. Label this last sentence as practice guidance, not a quotation from the Act.

### 5. Communication when a patient asks about VAD

#### Evidence-informed first-conversation structure

1. **Acknowledge and make space.** Recognise that raising VAD may have been difficult and respond without shock, argument or premature eligibility judgment. [S9]
2. **Clarify the words and intent.** Ask an open question such as “Can you tell me more about what you are asking for?” Explicit mention of VAD, assisted dying, euthanasia or medication to hasten death may be clear; statements such as “I’m tired of life” require clarification. [S9]
3. **Explore what is driving the request.** Ask about symptoms, function, fears, control, family or carer strain, cultural and spiritual concerns, depression or other distress, and gaps in care. This exploration supports care and does not invalidate the request. [S2]
4. **Check whether this is information-seeking or a formal first request.** If the criteria in s 19 are met, switch from an exploratory conversation to the required response, record and Board notification workflow. [S1; S4]
5. **Be transparent about role and limits.** State whether the GP participates, does not participate, or needs to check eligibility to act. Do not imply that personal objection is a clinical finding about the patient. [S9; S10]
6. **Explain options without false equivalence.** In NSW, a clinician who raises VAD must cover treatment, outcomes and palliative care in the same discussion. Even when the patient raises it, good care includes ensuring symptoms and palliative needs are addressed. [S1; S2; S4]
7. **Protect continuity.** Explicitly reassure the patient that the therapeutic relationship and ordinary care will continue, regardless of the GP’s VAD role. [S1; S9]
8. **Offer a concrete next step.** Give or help contact the Care Navigator Service, arrange palliative or symptom review, address urgent mental health or safety concerns through usual pathways, and plan follow-up. [S4; S7–S9]
9. **Document factually.** Record the patient’s words, clarification, whether a first request occurred, information given, the GP’s decision, referrals or connections offered and the follow-up plan. [S1; S2; S9]

#### Suggested teaching language (interpretation, not statutory wording)

> “Thank you for telling me. I’d like to understand what you mean and what has been hardest. Are you asking for information about voluntary assisted dying, or are you making a request to access it? We can also make sure your symptoms, palliative care and other supports are addressed. I’ll explain what I can do, and your usual care will continue.”

### 6. Relationship to adjacent areas of care

#### Palliative care

- VAD is a distinct legal intervention and is not itself palliative care. They are not mutually exclusive. A patient can receive palliative care before, during and after a VAD application, and choosing VAD must not remove access to other care. [S2; S13]
- The 2024–25 Board report found that 87.6% of patients who had a first assessment during that reporting year were receiving palliative care at that time. This is descriptive service data, not proof that every applicant must accept palliative care. [S11]
- Refusing life-sustaining treatment and proportionate symptom relief are not VAD. [S2]

#### Advance care planning

- Advance care planning remains important, including for a person pursuing VAD, because it can guide other care if the person later loses capacity. [S2]
- An advance care directive cannot be used to request or authorise VAD after loss of capacity. A substitute decision-maker cannot continue the VAD process on the person’s behalf. [S1; S2; S13]

#### Capacity assessment

- VAD-specific decision-making capacity is defined by the Act and is decision-specific. The person must understand, retain as necessary, weigh and communicate the relevant VAD information and decision. Capacity is presumed unless shown otherwise. [S1, s 6]
- Diagnosis of dementia or mental illness does not by itself establish incapacity. Conversely, a diagnosis does not remove the need for careful assessment or supported communication. [S1; S2]
- Where an authorised assessor cannot decide capacity, the Act requires referral to a psychiatrist or another appropriately skilled registered health practitioner. [S1, s 27]

#### Suicide-risk assessment

- A request made and action taken in accordance with the Act is not suicide for NSW law. It should not automatically be relabelled as a suicidal crisis. [S1, s 12]
- This does **not** mean clinicians should ignore acute risk. Ambiguous statements such as “I just want to die”, evidence of a plan outside the VAD framework, severe distress or immediate danger require clarification and ordinary clinical safety assessment and care. [S6; S9]
- Teaching interpretation: hold two possibilities without conflating them—an informed request to discuss a lawful end-of-life option, and co-existing or alternative suicidal distress that needs assessment. The legal label does not replace clinical judgement, and a mental health assessment must not be used as an automatic barrier where the Act’s referral criteria are not engaged.

### 8. Bathurst-specific pathway

Bathurst is within WNSWLHD. No public source located identifies a named Bathurst VAD practitioner, and a named practitioner should not be invented or inferred. The verified pathway is service-based:

1. **Start with the patient’s usual doctor or treating team.** The patient—not a family member or referrer—must make any formal first request to a medical practitioner during a consultation. A GP can still offer a warm connection with the patient’s consent. [S1; S4]
2. **Use the WNSWLHD VAD Navigation Service.** Phone **1800 684 283**, Monday–Friday **9 am–3 pm**, excluding public holidays. The service states it can provide general information and coordinate appointments with a VAD practitioner for a person who wishes to progress a request. [S8]
3. **Use the statewide service if preferred or if local contact is unavailable.** NSW VAD Care Navigator Service: **1300 802 133**, Monday–Friday **8:30 am–4:30 pm**, excluding public holidays; **NSLHD-VADCareNavigator@health.nsw.gov.au**. It can answer questions and help find an authorised practitioner. [S7]
4. **Expect a mix of local, outreach and face-to-face care.** NSW’s statewide Access Service supports regional equity, but some steps or administration-related discussions must occur face to face because of clinical requirements and Commonwealth carriage-service risk. Do not promise that the entire process can occur by telehealth. [S2; S3]
5. **Continue parallel care.** Maintain GP, specialist, palliative care, symptom management, psychosocial, cultural and mental health support while the VAD service manages the statutory process. [S2; S4]
6. **Consider travel support without promising it.** IPTAAS may assist with VAD-related travel and accommodation when its eligibility and distance criteria are met. [S14]

Service activity confirms that the pathway has been used by Bathurst residents: the Board recorded 19 people residing in Bathurst Regional LGA who had a first assessment in 2024–25. This does not establish current practitioner availability or predict an individual’s eligibility or waiting time. [S11]

## Teaching interpretation separated from researched claims

The following are proposed teaching choices rather than direct legal rules:

- Begin with listening and clarification before process explanation, unless immediate safety needs take priority.
- Teach the “trigger point” between a broad conversation and a formal first request because the legal duties change at that point.
- Present conscientious objection as a boundary around participation, not a boundary around compassion, information access or continuing ordinary care.
- Show VAD, palliative care and advance care planning as parallel but distinct pathways rather than competing options.
- Use the Bathurst pathway as a navigation exercise, not as a promise of a particular practitioner, timeframe or outcome.
- Avoid method, dose, drug-name or administration-instruction content in the public lesson.
